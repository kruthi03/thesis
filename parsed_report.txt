ABSTRACT
It is widely recognized that Security Information and Event Management systems are essential for detecting anomalous behavior across large volumes of log data. However, the resulting alerts are often too technical and voluminous for analysts to interpret quickly. To address this, Security Operations Centers increasingly rely on Large Language Models to translate raw anomaly signals into natural-language explanations. Although these narratives are fast to read and improve situational awareness, they are prone to hallucination, producing confident claims that fabricate, or misattribute the underlying evidence. In security contexts, unverified hallucination is not a flaw but an operational risk, capable of misleading triage decisions or manipulating analyst attention independently of the evidence itself.
Despite growing industry adoption of LLM-generated explanations in security workflows, there is no commonly accepted method for guaranteeing their factual correctness. Existing approaches to hallucination mitigation improve productivity but do not provide measurable, statistically valid accuracy guarantees.
The primary objective of this thesis is to address this gap by combining conformal prediction with a log-grounded factuality check to provide sentence-level guarantees on the correctness of LLM-generated anomaly explanations, without requiring retraining of the underlying language model.
The study utilizes structured anomaly signals derived from insider-threat log data, pairing multiple LLM-generated narrative samples per anomaly with sentence-level factuality labels. The thesis outlines the development of a dual-signal admission function that combines natural language inference scoring with deterministic numeric and entity fact-matching to assess whether each generated sentence is factually supported by its underlying evidence. 
Since the underlying anomaly signals and human factuality labels are derived from a single dataset, the calibrated guarantees are conditional on the specific rendering, model, and pooling configuration used to generate them, and further validation against additional log sources and generative models would strengthen the generality of the claims. For future research, extending the admission function with a fact-binding check and validating the pipeline against production SIEM telemetry would improve robustness beyond the current experimental setting.

Chapter 1 Introduction
This chapter introduces the operational context, theoretical motivations, and technical challenges surrounding automated anomaly explanations in enterprise security environments. It begins by establishing the broad landscape of insider threats and the evolution of data driven defensive architectures, subsequently narrowing to the critical problem of verifiable factual hallucination in Large Language Models (LLMs). By introducing empirical evidence of specific hallucination failure modes most notably, arithmetic derivation and contextual mis weighting this chapter isolates the precise research gap addressed by this thesis. Finally, it outlines the specific research questions to be answered, summarizes the non monotone conformal prediction methodology employed, explicitly states the academic contributions to the field, defines the study’s limitations, and provides a structural roadmap for the subsequent chapters.
1.1 Background and Context
The insider threat remains among the most persistent and damaging security challenges faced the modern enterprise. Predating digital systems entirely, the core dilemma of the insider threat rests on a fundamental paradox any individual granted legitimate access to sensitive organizational resources carries a risk profile that is inherently different from that of an external attacker. The very credentials, privileges, and network access that enable employees, contractors, and business partners to perform their legitimate duties are the exact tools required to compromise the system. Digital transformation has magnified this risk exponentially rather than resolving it. Modern workforces operate continuously within distributed, highly interconnected information systems where malicious actions such as targeted digital sabotage, and unauthorized data disclosure can be completely indistinguishable from ordinary, benign work activity until catastrophic damage has already occurred. Industry estimates consistently place the cost of a single confirmed insider incident in the millions of dollars once forensic investigation, operational downtime, remediation efforts, and regulatory exposure are fully accounted for.
This immense financial and operational cost has driven sustained, aggressive investment in continuous detection infrastructure across the cybersecurity industry. SIEM platforms have emerged over the last two decades to meet this need at an enterprise scale. SIEMs function by aggregating security relevant telemetry spanning authentication logs, file system interactions, email metadata, web proxy activity, and removable device usage from across an organization's sprawling digital footprint into a single, centralized data store.
However, raw data collection alone is insufficient for threat detection. User and Entity Behavior Analytics (UEBA) was developed as the critical analytics layer built directly on top of this aggregated SIEM data. Rather than collecting new information, UEBA algorithms analyze the historical logs that a SIEM has already gathered to construct a highly specific behavioral baseline for every individual user and endpoint entity. When current activity drifts significantly beyond this established statistical baseline, the UEBA system flags the behavior as anomalous. This approach has matured considerably in recent years. Unsupervised machine learning models and complex rule based detection heuristics which require no prior labeled examples of attack behavior are now the industry standard. This unsupervised reliance is a mathematical necessity confirmed insider incidents are exceptionally rare relative to the astronomical volume of benign daily corporate activity, rendering traditional supervised training methodologies unreliable due to severe class imbalance.
Figure 1.1: UEBA workflow
Despite these advancements in detection engineering, this analytical layer has fundamentally failed to solve the interpretability problem. When a UEBA system flags a user's activity for a given week, the output typically arrives at the Security Operations Center (SOC) merely as a mathematical anomaly score, a raw binary flag, or a triggered rule identifier. It acts as a statistical signal that something is suspicious but provides zero narrative account of what transpired or why the deviation matters contextually. An entry level SOC analyst receiving such a flag must manually open a raw log viewer, pivot across multiple indices, locate the relevant timestamped activity, and attempt to reconstruct a behavioral timeline from potentially hundreds of individuals, disjointed events before confidently deciding whether the alert warrants a severe incident escalation.
This investigative process is painstakingly slow, cognitively demanding, and is heavily documented as a primary driver of alert fatigue. Analysts, desensitized by overwhelming volumes of opaque, unexplained statistical flags, begin to systematically deprioritize them, inevitably allowing genuine, sophisticated threats to pass uninvestigated. Recently, the security industry has turned to Large Language Models (LLMs) as a highly visible, automated remedy. Given the structured telemetry evidence behind a UEBA alert, an LLM can leverage its semantic fluency to automatically generate a readable, natural language narrative. This narrative explains what the user did and why the pattern deviates from their baseline, theoretically converting a dense spreadsheet of raw events into an actionable paragraph that an analyst can triage in seconds.
The problem and the fundamental starting point of this thesis is that while these generative paragraphs are highly fluent, they are inherently decoupled from factual accuracy.
1.2 Problem Description
Modern Security Operations Centers (SOCs) must continuously monitor and defend enterprise environments against increasingly sophisticated cyber threats. To manage this vast volume of data, organizations rely heavily on UEBA and SIEM systems. These advanced analytical systems excel at identifying statistical deviations and detecting anomalous behaviors from massive troves of raw log data. However, while UEBA generates rich and highly precise anomaly signals, these systems inherently struggle to deliver human readable explanations at a speed that matches analyst workflows. Current SIEM and UEBA research heavily prioritizes anomaly detection but typically stops at presenting raw technical signals. This creates a severe operational bottleneck, neglecting the crucial explanation layer where human comprehension ultimately determines operational effectiveness. 
In a typical deployment, SOCs process thousands of complex anomaly alerts every single day. The raw, log based format of these technical alerts routinely overwhelms analysts, especially given the severe time constraints inherent in critical incident response scenarios. As analysts are bombarded with technical signals that lack semantic structure, they experience severe alert fatigue. This cognitive overload directly compromises response times and diminishes the overall efficacy of the security team. Consequently, while the mathematical detection of an anomaly is largely a solved prerequisite, the translation of that complex technical alert into an actionable format remains a critical, unsolved challenge in cybersecurity operations. 
To bridge this interpretability gap and accelerate the incident triage process, the cybersecurity industry has increasingly turned to Large Language Models (LLMs). LLMs can simplify massive volumes of complex log data into natural language summaries, delivering explanations that dramatically improve SOC productivity. Discussions at recent industry forums highlight a rapid transition toward the  agentic SOC  paradigm, where industry leaders integrate LLMs and specialized AI agents to automate the full alert lifecycle, from initial data enrichment to alert summarization. However, these systems focus overwhelmingly on orchestration speed and productivity, fundamentally failing to provide formal guarantees regarding the factuality of the LLM's explanations. 
This prioritization of speed over factual guarantees introduces a severe operational vulnerability. Although an LLM generated summary is extremely fast and fluent to read, single LLM outputs inherently carry a high risk of hallucination. In high stakes security operations, where factual accuracy is absolutely required to ensure organizational safety, these persistent hallucination risks thoroughly undermine trust in the generated outputs. If an analyst acts upon a rapidly generated but factually incorrect explanation, the resulting errors could compromise organizational security, lead to misallocated response resources, or cause legitimate threats to be dangerously dismissed. 
Despite widespread concerns regarding hallucinations in high stakes applications, current academic and industrial approaches to mitigation lack statistical rigor. Existing hallucination mitigation techniques such as utilizing an LLM as a judge, measuring semantic entropy, or relying on logit based calibration and uncertainty estimates suffer from severe structural limitations. These methods either require access to proprietary model internals that are strictly unavailable when using production black box LLMs, or they simply fail to provide the distribution free, finite sample error guarantees that are essential for reliable SOC deployment. Furthermore, existing conformal abstention methodologies that provide document level guarantees are practically inefficient, as rejecting an entire document wastes perfectly correct sentences contained within it. These current methods may improve heuristic filtering, but they fail to deliver verifiable factual reliability for multi component UEBA explanations. 
Consequently, a critical research gap exists no current work successfully combines log grounded admission filtering with sentence level conformal selection for production UEBA explanations. This thesis operates directly at the intersection of cybersecurity operations, LLM hallucination mitigation, and conformal prediction to address this limitation. By applying conformal prediction as a statistical safety net, this research transforms raw AI narratives into verifiably trustworthy explanations. Specifically, this thesis aims to deliver a log grounded conformal prediction pipeline that operates at sentence level granularity. This methodology provides distribution free, finite sample validity without the necessity of retraining the underlying language model, ultimately allowing SOC analysts to balance explanation utility against factual risk by mathematically bounding error rates at user configurable levels.
1.3 Research Questions
To bridge this gap, this thesis investigates whether non monotone conformal prediction can mathematically bound complex hallucination risks in automated security narratives. The central inquiry is systematically decomposed into the following three core research questions
RQ1 Can a log grounded anomaly signal, in which every potential fact a narrative could reference is forced into an enumerable, typed, and numeric format, reliably support the sentence level verification of subsequently generated natural language narratives?
RQ2 Can a dual signal admission function combining Natural Language Inference (NLI) self consistency across repeated language model samples with strict numeric/entity fact matching against structured log evidence be combined into a deterministic rule that successfully distinguishes grounded sentences from hallucinated, mis weighted, and falsely precise claims with usable precision and recall?
RQ3 Can conformal component selection, specifically calibrated over a non monotone, false discovery shaped loss using the Learn Then Test (LTT) framework, provide a formal, finite sample statistical guarantee on the fraction of factually incorrect sentences admitted to an analyst, at a user chosen risk level , without requiring any retraining of the underlying language model?
1.4 Approach
This thesis designs and implements an end to end pipeline that transforms raw security telemetry into statistically controlled, natural language explanations of UEBA alerts. The methodology treats the anomaly detector as a fixed prerequisite and focuses entirely on the explainability and verification layers. The implementation is organised as a unidirectional, multi stage workflow in Python, combining structured data processing, asynchronous language model inference, deterministic linguistic verification, supervised scoring, and non monotone conformal risk control.
Stage 1: Signal extraction
Telemetry from the CERT Insider Threat Test Dataset r6.2 is aggregated at user week level and processed using five transparent detection rules: after hours activity spikes, elevated removable media usage, high volume external email with attachments, off role browsing (including leak site visits), and peer relative deviation. These rules act as a stand in for a deployed UEBA detector. Each flagged user week is transformed into a structured anomaly signal containing strictly enumerable, typed numeric facts. Facts are cleanly separated into self relative baselines (a user’s trailing history), peer relative baselines (role matched comparators), and specific anomalous evidence. This enumerable fact representation ensures that every claim a narrative might make corresponds to a verifiable element in the signal.
Stage 2: Narrative generation
A locally hosted, open weight language model treats the structured signal as a prompt and generates independent narrative explanations per signal. The model operates at a non zero sampling temperature (). This stochastic multi sampling produces a diverse distribution of candidate sentences, deliberately exploiting variance across samples to expose the model’s internal uncertainty rather than suppressing it. Generated text is segmented into discrete sentences and pooled across samples via semantic clustering. The frequency of each semantic cluster provides a self consistency score used later in admission scoring.
Stage 3: Sentence scoring and admission
Candidate sentences are evaluated by a deterministic admission function that calculates a confidence score by merging two independent signals. The first is an NLI based self consistency score (using a DeBERTa cross encoder) to evaluate semantic agreement across sampled narratives. The second is a regex driven numeric and entity fact matching script tied directly to the Stage 1 structured evidence. Crucially, this function includes a rigid heuristic override: if a sentence inappropriately frames a background context fact as the driving cause of the alert (misweighting), its score is forced to zero, regardless of numerical accuracy. This reflects the security domain requirement that correct values must also be assigned to the correct causal explanation.
Stage 4: Conformal calibration
The pipeline requires statistical calibration to guarantee error rates. Because the error loss function is non monotone (specifically, the False Discovery Rate of incorrect sentences changes its denominator depending on how many sentences are accepted), standard conformal prediction fails. Therefore, this stage employs the Learn Then Test (LTT) framework. Utilising Hoeffding–Bentkus  values and fixed sequence  investing, the system calibrates an acceptance threshold over a human annotated, user level exchangeable split to strictly control the expected false discovery rate. The calibrated threshold is selected only after the calibration procedure has been completed on a dedicated split.
Stage 5: Evaluation
The calibrated threshold is subsequently applied to a rigidly held out test set to measure empirical risk against the nominal user defined target risk (). This evaluates both statistical validity and practical usefulness (recall). Evaluation metrics include empirical false discovery proportion, precision and recall of the admission function, recall of grounded content retained after filtering, and the proportion of candidate sentences admitted. Baseline comparisons include an uncalibrated threshold and an NLI only ablation that excludes deterministic fact matching. All explanation level results are reported conditional on successful upstream anomaly detection to decouple detection failures from verification failures.
Finally, an Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI) simulation is executed using a logistic regression update model to evaluate how dynamic threshold adjustments perform under the chronological drift inherent to long term security telemetry. This experiment assesses whether dynamic threshold adjustment can respond to changing narrative quality and maintain an appropriate balance between factual safety and operational recall.
1.5 Thesis Contribution
This research bridges the isolated fields of cybersecurity explainability and conformal risk control. While each constituent technique is individually established, their combination and specific application to structured security telemetry rather than general domain text has not been previously demonstrated. The thesis delivers concrete contributions.
Log derived conformal representation. The work introduces an enumerable fact anomaly representation that pairs raw CERT r6.2 telemetry with LLM generated narratives. Every potential claim a narrative might make corresponds to a typed, numeric fact in the source signal. This provides a concrete, structured dataset specifically designed for security explainability and verification research, enabling deterministic grounding checks that are impossible with unstructured logs or free text references.
Misweighting aware admission function. The thesis designs, implements, and empirically evaluates an admission function that combines NLI based self consistency with strict numeric and entity fact matching. Crucially, it incorporates a dedicated misweighting detector that identifies sentences which inappropriately frame background context (such as role or department) as the causal driver of an anomaly. Ablation experiments demonstrate that standard LLM self consistency alone is insufficient for security text and that deterministic fact matching and misweighting overrides are necessary in high stakes operational environments.
Sentence level conformal selection for security. The work adapts the non monotone Learn Then Test (LTT) calibration framework to sentence granularity selection in a strictly log grounded setting. This extends existing conformal abstention methodologies beyond standard open domain question answering tasks into the rigid domain of security verification. The implementation shows how false discovery rate control can be achieved at sentence level without retraining the underlying language model, allowing SOC analysts to balance explanation utility against factual risk at user configurable levels.
Empirical hallucination taxonomy. The thesis formalises a detailed, empirically derived failure taxonomy encompassing fact mixing, category conflation, arithmetic derivation, and contextual misweighting. This taxonomy is derived from direct manual annotation of real generated security narratives rather than assumed a priori. It provides a security specific refinement of the standard intrinsic–extrinsic hallucination distinction and documents failure modes that general domain decompose then verify frameworks do not capture.
Together, these contributions establish a verifiable, log grounded explanation pipeline for UEBA alerts. The approach demonstrates that language model narratives can be transformed from fluent but untrusted outputs into statistically controlled evidence suitable for high stakes security operations.
1.6 Limitations
The findings of this study are bounded by several practical and theoretical constraints. These limitations are rooted in specific, documented properties of the underlying data and methodology, and represent accepted engineering trade offs rather than methodological oversights.
First, all empirical results are derived from a single synthetic corpus the CERT r6.2 dataset. While highly structured and standard within the literature, its synthetic provenance and the limited absolute number of ground truth insider incidents restrict the statistical power of any massive calibration claim. 
Methodologically, the behavioral features within the pipeline are aggregated at the user week level. This is a deliberate constraint designed to keep temporal claims narratable and checkable, but it cannot resolve anomalies concentrated entirely within a single hour of an otherwise ordinary week. Finally, the language model utilized for Stage 2 generation is a comparatively small, locally hosted open weight model. This selection was made explicitly to prioritize data sovereignty a mandatory requirement in enterprise SOCs rather than to maximize narrative fluency, resulting in a higher baseline rate of the  derived number  failures that the system must catch.

Chapter 2 Theory and State of the Art
As the complexity and volume of enterprise network telemetry have grown exponentially, the mechanisms used to secure these environments have undergone a forced evolution from static, signature based defenses to dynamic, behavior driven analytics. However, this evolution has generated a secondary crisis of interpretability. Modern anomaly detection systems are highly proficient at identifying statistical deviations, yet they are structurally incapable of explaining them. Consequently, security analysts are burdened with translating abstract mathematical anomalies into actionable human narratives, a process that introduces severe operational latency. While Large Language Models (LLMs) possess the semantic fluency required to bridge this gap, their tendency to confidently hallucinate facts renders their unverified deployment in Security Operations Centers (SOCs) an unacceptable risk.
This chapter establishes the theoretical foundations and reviews the state of the art required to contextualize, design, and validate a verifiable, log grounded explanation pipeline. The chapter is structured into four distinct parts. Part 1 (Section 2.1 through 2.3) provides a rigorous architectural breakdown of the foundational technologies underpinning this thesis Security Information and Event Management systems, User and Entity Behavior Analytics , and the Transformer based architecture of LLMs. Part 2 transitions into the methodologies of semantic validation, Natural Language Inference (NLI) mechanisms and cross encoder architectures. Part 3 introduces the mathematical framework of Conformal Prediction, contrasting split conformal methods with the non monotone Learn Then Test (LTT) procedures required for sentence level risk bounding. Finally, Part 4 synthesizes the contemporary literature surrounding cybersecurity explainability and LLM hallucination mitigation, explicitly identifying the prevailing research gap the absence of formal, finite sample statistical guarantees for security log grounded natural language generation. Together, these sections establish the theoretical scaffolding upon which the pipeline in Chapter 4 is built.
2.1 Security Information and Event Management (SIEM)
2.1.1 Historical Evolution and Architectural Purpose
The notion of Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) arose in the early 2000s as a crucial consolidation of two distinct disciplines Security Information Management (SIM), which focused on storage for long term and analysis of log data, and Security Event Management (SEM), which focused on real time monitoring and correlation of security alerts (Kent & Souppaya, 2006). Prior to the advent of SIEM, network administrators monitored isolated security appliances such as firewalls, Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS), and antivirus servers through disparate consoles (Kent & Souppaya, 2006). As enterprise architecture scaled, this decentralized approach resulted in isolated data silos, making it impossible to trace advanced persistent threats (APTs) moving laterally across a network.
The primary architectural purpose of a SIEM is to serve as the centralized nervous system of the Security Operations Center. By ingesting, normalizing, and correlating telemetry from thousands of endpoints, proxies, and authentication servers, the SIEM transforms raw machine data into a unified timeline of enterprise activity (Bhatt et al., 2020).
2.1.2 Rule Based Correlation and its Limitations
Historically, the analytical engine of a SIEM relied exclusively on rule based correlation. Security engineers write deterministic Boolean logic to detect known attack signatures or prohibited state transitions (Bace & Mell, 2001). A classic correlation rule might dictate IF (Failed Login Attempts > 5 within 1 Minute) AND (Subsequent Successful Login = True), THEN Trigger Alert.
While rule based correlation is computationally efficient and highly interpretable an analyst knows exactly which rule triggered and why it suffers from two fatal limitations. First, it is strictly reactive. Rules can only be written for attack vectors that are already known and perfectly understood. Second, rule based systems scale poorly against legitimate operational variance. A threshold that defines  abnormal download volume  for a marketing employee will generate endless false positives if applied to a database administrator. Attempting to manage thousands of highly specific, role based correlation rules results in immense administrative overhead and the eventual degradation of the SOC's efficacy due to alert fatigue (Al Mhiqani et al., 2020). The failure of static rules to adapt to dynamic human behavior necessitated the evolution of behavioral analytics.
2.2 User and Entity Behavior Analytics (UEBA)
2.2.1 From Signatures to Behavioral Baselines
To address the brittleness of deterministic SIEM rules, User and Entity Behavior Analytics (UEBA) was introduced to shift the detection paradigm from signature matching to statistical anomaly detection (Nurse et al., 2014). Rather than asking  Does this event match a known bad signature? , UEBA asks  Does this event significantly deviate from the historical norm for this specific user or their peer group? 
Figure 2.1 User and Entity Behavior Analysis Workflow
This paradigm shift requires the system to maintain continuous, dynamic state. A UEBA engine aggregates raw normalized logs into multi dimensional feature vectors representing entity behavior over specific time windows (e.g., days or weeks). The system then computes statistical baselines, allowing it to detect  unknown unknowns  novel attack vectors or insider threats that have no pre existing signature, but which mathematically disrupt the established behavioral equilibrium (Salem, Hershkop, & Stolfo, 2008).
2.2.2 Statistical Profiling and Distance Metrics
The most foundational algorithm in UEBA is the computation of trailing statistical baselines. For continuous or discrete count features (such as total megabytes transferred or total HTTP requests made), the system computes measures of central tendency and dispersion.
To avoid the impact of powerful outliers on the baseline itself, robust statistics are preferred over standard mean and variance. A standard UEBA implementation computes the trailing Median and the Median Absolute Deviation (MAD). The anomaly score for a user's feature at time is frequently formulated as a modified z score
However, in highly sparse enterprise telemetry, a user may exhibit a history of exactly zero events for a specific action (e.g., zero visits to a leak publication website over 12 weeks). In this scenario, both the Median and MAD equal zero. A single subsequent visit results in a division by zero, yielding an infinite anomaly score. Modern UEBA pipelines, including the extraction stage implemented in this thesis, apply mathematical floors to the dispersion metric to prevent these zero variance explosions. By enforcing a Poisson inspired spread floor such as the system safely bounds the anomaly score, allowing novel events to be flagged without destroying the numerical stability of the feature matrix.
Furthermore, UEBA systems implement peer group baselining. By utilizing directory services (e.g., LDAP), the system groups users by department or role. A user's activity is compared not only to their own history (the self z score) but also to the concurrent median of their role cohort (the peer z score). This dual baseline architecture allows the system to suppress false positives caused by company wide events (which elevate the peer median simultaneously) and to detect localized insider threats where a user deviates sharply from colleagues performing identical roles.
2.2.3 Unsupervised Machine Learning in UEBA
Beyond univariate statistical profiling, modern UEBA leverages multivariate unsupervised machine learning to detect anomalies across high dimensional feature spaces (Greitzer et al., 2014Le & Zincir Heywood, 2021).
Isolation Forests are extensively deployed in UEBA due to their efficiency with high dimensional, continuous data (Liu, Ting, & Zhou, 2008). Unlike algorithms that attempt to profile normal behavior, Isolation Forests explicitly isolate anomalies. The algorithm recursively partitions the dataset by randomly selecting a feature and a split value. Because anomalies are  few and different,  they require significantly fewer random partitions to be isolated into their own terminal leaf nodes. The anomaly score increases as the distance from the root to the leaf decreases shorter paths indicate highly anomalous behavior.
Figure 2.2  Isolation Forest Anomaly Detection
Autoencoders (neural networks designed to reconstruct their input) are similarly utilized. The network compresses the user's behavioral vector into a low dimensional bottleneck layer and then attempts to reconstruct the original vector. The network is trained exclusively on historical (presumed normal) data. When an anomalous behavioral vector is processed, the network fails to accurately reconstruct the novel patterns, resulting in a high Mean Squared Error (MSE) reconstruction loss. This loss value serves directly as the continuous anomaly score.

2.2.4 The Alert Interpretability Gap
While UEBA successfully detects novel insider threats that evade static rules, it introduces a severe operational bottleneck. When an Isolation Forest or an Autoencoder flags a user week as malicious, the output is an abstract, multi dimensional distance metric.
The unsupervised machine learning algorithm cannot explain whether the high score was driven by abnormal USB activity, unusual login times, or a subtle, complex combination of both. Consequently, the SOC analyst must manually query the SIEM, visually scanning hundreds of raw log lines to reconstruct the narrative of the anomaly. This interpretation gap negates the speed advantages of automated detection, establishing the critical need for automated, natural language explanation layers.
2.3 Large Language Models (LLMs) and Autoregressive Generation
To bridge the interpretability gap generated by UEBA, the security industry has turned to Large Language Models. To understand both the capabilities and the inherent risks of deploying LLMs as anomaly narrators, one must examine their underlying neural architecture and generation mechanics.
2.3.1 The Transformer Architecture and Self Attention
Natural language processing relied heavily on Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short Term Memory (LSTM) networks, which processed text sequentially. This sequential processing prevented parallelization and severely limited the model's ability to retain context over long text passages.

The Transformer discarded recurrence entirely in favor of the Self Attention mechanism. Self attention allows the model to compute a weighted representation of a sequence by explicitly measuring the semantic relevance of every word (token) to every other word in the input, regardless of their physical distance in the sentence.
Mathematically, the input sequence is projected into three distinct matrices Queries (), Keys (), and Values (), utilizing learned weight matrices. The attention scores are computed by taking the dot product of the Queries and Keys, scaling by the square root of the hidden dimension depth () to prevent vanishing gradients, applying a softmax function to create a probability distribution, and multiplying the result by the Values matrix

This mechanism allows the LLM to process a structured UEBA anomaly signal (e.g., a list of numeric facts) and dynamically attend to the relevant evidence and context parameters when constructing the subsequent explanation, ensuring that the generated verbs and subjects align with the provided data.
2.3.2 Autoregressive Text Generation
Models such as GPT, LLaMA, and Gemini utilize the decoder only variant of the Transformer architecture (Brown et al., 2020). These models are trained via causal language modeling they are optimized to predict the probability of the next token in a sequence given all preceding tokens.
During inference (the generation of an anomaly explanation), the LLM operates autoregressively. It receives the prompt (the structured UEBA signal and system instructions), computes the probability distribution for the first generated token, samples a token from that distribution, appends it to the input sequence, and repeats the process until a special stopping token is generated.
This token by token generation process is fundamentally probabilistic. The LLM does not possess a database of facts or an internal logic engine. It is a highly sophisticated statistical engine mapping input text geometries to likely output continuations. When tasked with explaining a security anomaly, the model relies on the semantic relationships learned during its pre training phase to construct fluent, professional prose.

2.4 Natural Language Inference (NLI) and Semantic Clustering
To automate the evaluation of an LLM's internal confidence, a system must be able to computationally determine whether distinct linguistic formulations assert the same underlying factual claim. This capability relies upon the theoretical frameworks of Natural Language Inference (NLI) and semantic clustering.
2.4.1 Theoretical Foundations of NLI
Natural Language Inference historically referred to as Recognizing Textual Entailment (RTE) is computational task of determining the directional logical relationship between two fragments of text a  premise  () and a  hypothesis  (). The fundamental objective is to classify the relationship into one of three mutually exclusive categories
Entailment If the premise is true, the hypothesis must necessarily be true.
Contradiction If the premise is true, the hypothesis must necessarily be false.
Neutral The truth of the hypothesis cannot be determined from the premise alone.
The formalization of this task catalyzed a massive shift in Natural Language Processing (NLP), driven by the release of large scale annotated datasets such as the Stanford Natural Language Inference (SNLI) corpus (Bowman et al., 2015) and Multi Genre NLI (Williams, Nangia, & Bowman, 2018). These datasets transitioned the field from fragile, rules based syntactic matching and rigid formal logic to deep, statistical representation learning. The core theoretical assumption is that a model capable of accurately resolving NLI tasks has developed a robust, generalizable representation of semantic meaning, entity coreference, and localized reasoning.

Bi encoders process the premise and the hypothesis independently through two separate Transformer networks (or the same network in two distinct passes). The network outputs a dense vector representation (embedding) for each sentence. The relationship between the sentences is subsequently determined by computing a simple mathematical distance metric between the two vectors, most commonly cosine similarity

The specific cross encoder architecture frequently utilized in state of the art verification pipelines is DeBERTa (Decoding enhanced BERT with Disentangled Attention) (He et al., 2021). Unlike standard BERT models, which add absolute position embeddings to word embeddings directly at the input layer, DeBERTa represents each word using two distinct vectors, one for its semantic content and one for its relative position.

Theoretically, this allows the model to distinguish between the grammatical function of a word based on its location and its inherent semantic meaning. For security narratives heavily reliant on numerical values and specific entity locations, this disentangled attention provides a significantly higher resolution for entailment classification compared to standard Transformer baselines.
2.4.4 Semantic Clustering
In addition to directional entailment, evaluation pipelines must frequently cluster generated sentences to reduce redundancy before human annotation or final presentation. Semantic clustering utilizes the continuous vector space generated by Bi encoders.
Given a pool of candidate sentences, the system computes the pairwise cosine similarity matrix. Sentences are grouped into semantic equivalence classes based on a predefined distance threshold (). Agglomerative clustering algorithms process the similarity matrix to form dense clusters where all intra cluster similarities exceed . This theoretical approach assumes that the dense embedding space is isotropic, meaning that semantic distance maps linearly to Euclidean or cosine distance a property specifically optimized during the contrastive training of Sentence BERT models (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019).
2.5 Conformal Prediction and Risk Control
While NLI provides a heuristic score of semantic confidence, heuristic scores are inherently uncalibrated. A SoftMax probability of 0.90 from a neural network does not strictly mean there is a 90% chance the prediction is correct deep neural networks are notoriously overconfident (Guo et al., 2017). To deploy LLMs in high stakes environments, these heuristic outputs must be translated into rigorous statistical guarantees. This is achieved through the mathematical framework of Conformal Prediction.
2.5.1 Historical Context and Exchangeability
Conformal Prediction (CONFORMAL PREDICTION) was pioneered by Vladimir Vovk, Alexander Gammerman, and Glenn Shafer in the early 2000s (Vovk, Gammerman, & Shafer, 2005). It was designed as a universal model agnostic framework for uncertainty quantification. Unlike Bayesian methods, which require assumptions about the underlying data distribution and a prior probability distribution, Conformal Prediction is entirely distribution free. It provides finite sample guarantees (meaning the mathematical bounds hold exactly for datasets of any size, not just asymptotically as the sample size approaches infinity).
Figure 2.7 Conformal Prediction
The single foundational assumption of Conformal Prediction is exchangeability. A sequence of random variables is called if permuting their order leaves the joint probability distribution unchanged. In practical terms, this means the future test data must be drawn from the same underlying distribution as the calibration data, and the specific ordering of the data points must not contain information. If the exchangeability assumption holds, conformal prediction guarantees that the error rate of the model will be strictly bound by a user specified level.
2.5.2 Split Conformal Prediction
The most computationally efficient variant of CONFORMAL PREDICTION is Split Conformal Prediction (Papadopoulos et al., 2002). The methodology partitions an available labeled dataset into a training set and a separate calibration set.

Once the nonconformity scores for the calibration set (size ) are computed, they are sorted in ascending order. To guarantee that the true label of a new, unseen test point will be contained in the predicted set with probability , the algorithm selects the empirical quantile of the calibration scores. 2.5.3 Conformal Risk Control (CRC)
Standard split conformal prediction was designed for classification (where the output is a prediction set of labels) and regression (where the output is a prediction interval). It guarantees set coverage the probability that the true label is within the output set.
However, in many modern AI applications, set coverage is the wrong metric. When filtering a list of LLM generated sentences, the goal is not to guarantee that the  true  sentence is in the list. The goal is to guarantee that the expected fraction of incorrect sentences in the list is below a specific threshold.
Conformal Risk Control (CRC), developed by Bates et al. (2021), extends conformal prediction to bound the expected value of an arbitrary loss function . The fundamental requirement of standard CRC is that the loss function must be monotonically decreasing with respect to the acceptance threshold .
Let  be a threshold, and the accepted set of items be . If raising  strictly shrinks the accepted set and thereby strictly reduces (or maintains) the total loss, the loss function is monotone. In this scenario, CRC utilizes the calibration set to find the smallest threshold  such that the empirical risk (the average loss over the calibration set) plus a finite sample penalty term is less than or equal to the target risk . Because the loss decreases monotonically, bounding the empirical risk directly bounds the true expected risk.
2.6 The Learn Then Test (LTT) Framework
The requirement for monotonicity in Conformal Risk Control presents a fatal mathematical blocker for evaluating generated text. In the context of anomaly explanations, the relevant loss metric is the False Discovery Rate (FDR) the fraction of admitted sentences that are factually incorrect.
FDR is inherently non monotone. Suppose a threshold  admits 10 sentences, 1 of which is wrong. The FDR is 10%. If the threshold is raised to , the system might reject 8 correct sentences and keep 1 correct sentence and 1 wrong sentence. The accepted set shrinks, but the FDR suddenly spikes to 50%. Because raising the threshold can arbitrarily increase the error fraction, standard CRC cannot be applied.
To solve the non monotone risk control problem, Angelopoulos et al. (2021) introduced the Learn Then Test (LTT) framework. LTT abandons the quantile search mechanics of standard conformal prediction in favor of rigorous, fixed sequence multiple hypothesis testing.

2.7 Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI)
2.7.1 The Temporal Distribution Shift Problem
The mathematical guarantees provided by split conformal prediction and LTT rely entirely on the absolute sanctity of the exchangeability assumption. The data used to calibrate the threshold must be drawn from the exact same underlying distribution as the future test data.
In enterprise security environments, the exchangeability assumption is frequently violated by temporal distribution shift (Quinonero Candela et al., 2008). Network architectures evolve, employee behaviors shift due to organizational changes, and LLM generative outputs exhibit concept drift over time. If a static conformal threshold is calibrated in January, the underlying probability distribution of anomalies and hallucinations may have shifted significantly by June. Under temporal distribution shift, the static loses its mathematical guarantee, and the true empirical risk may silently exceed the target .
2.7.2 Online Threshold Updates
To maintain valid statistical guarantees under distribution shift, Gibbs and Candes (2021) introduced Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI). ACI transforms conformal prediction from a static, offline calibration process into a dynamic, online learning algorithm.
Instead of computing a single threshold , ACI maintains a time varying threshold . At each time step  (e.g., each day or each batch of generated sentences), the system utilizes the current threshold  to filter the data. A human annotator (or a highly reliable automated proxy) subsequently evaluates the filtered output and computes the true empirical loss at time , .

2.8 Evaluation Metrics
To evaluate the efficacy of the sentence scoring admission function and the statistical validity of the conformal calibration pipeline, a precise set of evaluation metrics must be formalized. Standard machine learning metrics are insufficient when applied to generative text in high stakes operational environments. Consequently, the evaluation methodology relies on a combination of specific statistical risk functions, operational text generation metrics, and conformal properties.
2.8.1 The Dual Requirement Validity and Usefulness
A formal statistical guarantee is only meaningful when accompanied by a demonstration that the guarantee holds empirically on unseen data, and by a second, independent demonstration that the resulting filtered output remains practically useful. This evaluation framework is organized explicitly around this dual requirement, following the established principle in conformal prediction literature that error control alone is an incomplete evaluation criterion (Vovk, Gammerman, & Shafer, 2005Bates et al., 2021).
A trivial decision rule that admits nothing at all achieves perfect validity under any reasonable loss definition an empty set can contain no incorrect elements while providing zero operational value to an analyst. Consequently, neither property is treated as sufficient in isolation both are reported jointly across all experimental sweeps.
2.8.2 False Discovery Rate (FDR)

Traditional classification metrics treat all errors symmetrically. 2.8.3 Precision and Recall
While FDR measures the aggregate statistical risk of the admitted set, standard Precision and Recall remain critical for evaluating the raw heuristic power of the underlying admission function prior to conformal calibration.
Within this sentence level verification architecture, the confusion matrix elements are defined as follows
True Positive (TP) A factually grounded sentence that is correctly admitted by the scoring function.
False Positive (FP) An incorrect sentence (hallucinated, derived number, or mis weighted) that is erroneously admitted.
False Negative (FN) A factually grounded sentence that is inappropriately rejected by the scoring function.
Precision measures the purity of the admitted sentence pool
Recall measures the sensitivity of the admission function to retain valuable context
A theoretical limitation of Recall in generative text evaluation is that an LLM may produce multiple semantically equivalent sentences representing a single underlying telemetry fact. Rejecting redundant variants does not operationally harm the analyst if one representative sentence is admitted however, standard Recall penalizes this redundancy rejection, making it a conservative lower bound on operational utility.

2.8.7 Conditional Reporting and the Detection–Explanation Boundary
A final methodological principle governing this evaluation concerns the boundary between the upstream detection layer (the UEBA rules flagging anomalies) and the downstream explanation layer (the LLM narrative generation and verification pipeline).
Because detection recall varies across different insider threat attack profiles, reporting an unconditioned, pooled explanation recall would conflict with two distinct failure modes (i) the failure of the detection rule to flag a subtle anomaly, and (ii) the failure of the admission function to verify a generated narrative. Consequently, all explanation and verification metrics reported in this thesis are explicitly conditional on correct upstream detection, ensuring that limitations inherent to the detection heuristics are analytically decoupled from the verification framework.
2.9 Discussion and Synthesis of Prior Work
The transition of enterprise security monitoring from static logs to complex statistical models has been defined by a continuous tension between detection efficacy and human interpretability. An analysis of the existing literature reveals five distinct thematic approaches to solving this interpretability gap and addressing the reliability of subsequent explanations. This section evaluates these thematic clusters, identifies key competitor methodologies, and explicitly positions the current thesis against them.
2.9.1 Semantic Ontologies and Rule-Based Enrichment
In the early development of Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) architectures, researchers focused heavily on augmenting raw alerts with contextual metadata using Semantic Web technologies. 
(Staniford-Chen et al. 1999) established structural protocols for querying relational asset databases, serving as a competitor methodology for structural enrichment.
Strengths: This rule-based approach excelled at determinism and structural rigidity. By formalizing the environment, SIEM engines successfully appended verifiable environmental context (e.g., user department, server role) directly to correlation alerts without any risk of hallucinated metadata. Weaknesses: The primary limitation of semantic ontologies was their inability to scale dynamically. These systems fell short when encountering novel threat vectors or behavioral drift, as they required rigid, manually maintained knowledge graphs. They provided factual context but failed to generate fluid, human-readable chronological narratives. Connection to Current Work: This thesis extends the core philosophy of deterministic, ontological grounding but fundamentally differs in its application. While early ontology research used deterministic structures to trigger alerts, this research utilizes a structured, enumerable-fact representation explicitly as an independent verification layer to bound the output of a generative model.
2.9.2 Unsupervised Machine Learning and Post-Hoc XAI Feature Attribution
As User and Entity Behavior Analytics (UEBA) platforms largely replaced deterministic Boolean logic with high-dimensional unsupervised models, detection architectures became mathematically opaque. In response, cybersecurity research heavily investigated post-hoc Explainable AI (XAI) techniques. Competitor studies in this space extensively benchmarked these architectures. For example, (Le and Zincir-Heywood 2021) conducted a massive empirical evaluation comparing Autoencoders and Isolation Forests against the CERT dataset. Their work successfully demonstrated that reconstruction-error models could rank malicious insider behavior accurately without labeled training examples. To explain these anomalies, researchers frequently applied SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) and LIME (Ribeiro, Singh, & Guestrin, 2016) to extract feature importance scores.
Strengths: Unsupervised ML methods solved the scaling limitations of rule-based systems, successfully detecting  unknown unknowns  by identifying statistical deviations from established baselines. XAI techniques effectively identified which mathematical vectors contributed most to the anomaly score. Weaknesses: These methods fell profoundly short in operational triage environments. Feature-attribution methods provided mathematical visibility into model parameters (e.g., showing that a specific byte-count feature was highly weighted), but they structurally failed to deliver the causal, chronological narratives required for rapid human incident response. Connection to Current Work: Unlike prior approaches, the proposed pipeline challenges the assumption that feature attribution equals operational explainability. Rather than competing with the detection efficacy of Isolation Forests or Autoencoders, this research treats those unsupervised models as a fixed prerequisite layer. It extends their utility by translating their abstract, multi-dimensional distance metrics into formal, natural-language narratives.
2.9.3 Generative Language Modeling and  Agentic SOC  Workflows
To bridge the gap between mathematical feature attribution and human-readable narratives, recent industry and academic efforts shifted toward integrating Large Language Models (LLMs) into the SOC. Current industrial workflows, such as the competitor architectures heavily documented in recent technical reports deploy LLMs to ingest SIEM alerts, correlate access histories, and draft natural-language incident tickets. These competitor deployments often rely on basic Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and prompt-engineering heuristics.
Strengths: LLM-based approaches largely solved the semantic fluency gap. They demonstrated an unprecedented ability to rapidly synthesize massive volumes of disjointed logs into highly readable, professional incident summaries, drastically reducing the initial cognitive load on human analysts. Weaknesses: The fatal flaw of these unconstrained agentic architectures was their factual unreliability. Current industry deployments depend entirely on unverified heuristics  prompt instructions  to govern behavior. These approaches failed to provide any finite-sample statistical guarantees regarding the factual accuracy of the generated tickets, introducing dangerous operational vulnerabilities where hallucinations were treated as actionable intelligence. Connection to Current Work: In contrast to these unconstrained frameworks, this research directly challenges the prevailing industry practice of deploying unverified or purely heuristic-driven LLMs in SOC environments. It differs by rejecting the assumption that prompt engineering or standard RAG is sufficient for high-stakes security operations, opting instead to enforce strict statistical verification boundaries on the generative output.
2.9.4 Decompose-Then-Verify Frameworks for Hallucination Mitigation
Recognizing the dangers of unconstrained generation, the broader Natural Language Processing (NLP) community pivoted toward  decompose-then-verify  architectures to mitigate hallucinations. Foundational competitor studies in this domain include FActScore (Min et al., 2023).These frameworks processed long-form text by utilizing an LLM to decompose the narrative into atomic claims, subsequently verifying each claim against an external knowledge source using Natural Language Inference (NLI).
Strengths: These methodologies established that statement-level factuality verification was both technically feasible and measurably more accurate than holistic, response-level scoring. They successfully automated the detection of extrinsic hallucinations (fabricated facts) by forcing claims to match retrieved context. Weaknesses: These general-domain verification frameworks fell short when applied to highly structured, numerical cybersecurity telemetry. They structurally struggled to distinguish between arithmetically derived truths and falsely precise numerical hallucinations. Furthermore, because these frameworks were designed for open-domain text generation and question-answering settings respectively, they were not designed to detect domain-specific logical errors  specifically, the causal misweighting of background. Connection to Current Work: The current work builds on the decompose-then-verify paradigm but completely re-architects the verification heuristic. It differs from FActScore and RAGAS by abandoning pure LLM-based NLI evaluation in favor of a dual-signal admission function. This thesis explicitly incorporates deterministic regex matching for numerical grounding and a dedicated spaCy dependency parser to catch the causal misweighting errors that general-domain frameworks systematically miss.
2.9.5 Conformal Prediction Applications in Natural Language
To mathematically bound the heuristic errors of NLI verifiers, recent literature explored Conformal Prediction (Vovk et al., 2005) as a safety mechanism. Specifically, the introduction of Conformal Risk Control (Bates et al., 2021) and the Learn-Then-Test framework (Angelopoulos et al., 2021) provided the theoretical machinery to bound non-monotone loss geometries, such as the False Discovery Rate. Recent competitor studies applied conformal abstention methodologies to open-domain generative question-answering systems, allowing language models to selectively abstain from answering when confidence was low (Quach et al., 2023).
Strengths: Conformal prediction frameworks provided distribution-free, finite-sample statistical guarantees. They successfully allowed system architects to formally cap the expected error rate without requiring any retraining of the underlying language or inference models. Weaknesses: Existing literature restricted the application of LTT primarily to open-domain classification and question-answering tasks. In the limited instances where component-level selection was applied to generated text, the methodologies often required human gold-reference answers for calibration  a resource that simply does not exist for the continuous volume of novel anomalies encountered in enterprise cybersecurity telemetry. Connection to Current Work: This thesis directly extends the mathematical application of the Learn-Then-Test framework. It differs from prior work by adapting sentence-level conformal selection specifically to the strict domain of security verification, relying entirely on an enumerable, log-grounded fact set rather than human-authored reference narratives for calibration.
2.9.6 Synthesis and Research Gap
Despite immense advancements across unsupervised anomaly detection, generative language modeling, and statistical risk control, the contemporary literature reveals a fractured landscape. UEBA platforms achieved advanced statistical detection capabilities but fundamentally lacked explainability. Unconstrained LLMs bridged this semantic gap but introduced unacceptable risks of factual fabrication and causal misweighting, which general-domain decompose-then-verify frameworks (like FActScore) failed to adequately resolve for numeric telemetry. Concurrently, while the Learn-Then-Test conformal framework offered the exact mathematical guarantees required for high-stakes environments, its application was isolated to open-domain tasks reliant on human reference text. Consequently, no existing work successfully combines log-grounded admission filtering with non-monotone conformal selection for production UEBA explanations. This thesis fills this precise research gap by constructing a sentence-level conformal prediction pipeline that verifies candidate narratives against structured anomaly signals  bounding hallucination rates at user-configurable limits without requiring proprietary model retraining or human reference texts.

3.1 Data Provenance and Context (The CERT r6.2 Dataset)
To evaluate the proposed conformal prediction architecture within a realistic Security Operations Center (SOC) context, this research utilizes the CERT Insider Threat Test Dataset, specifically release r6.2 (Glasser & Lindauer, 2013). Empirical validation of User and Entity Behavior Analytics (UEBA) systems faces a fundamental hurdle: real world corporate security logs are heavily encumbered by privacy constraints, personally identifiable information (PII), and strict non disclosure agreements. Furthermore, real world datasets rarely contain verified, labeled ground truth for insider threats. The CERT r6.2 dataset resolves this by providing a synthetic, densely parameterized, and highly realistic alternative that has become a benchmark standard in cybersecurity research. It simulates the digital footprint of a complex enterprise, containing detailed organizational structures (including supervisor subordinate hierarchies and departmental affiliations) and longitudinal activity logs for exactly 4,000 simulated employees over a continuous operational period of 516 days.
The dataset captures a holistic view of enterprise behavior through five primary raw log modalities:
Logon Events: Authentication records tracking interactive user sessions, capturing temporal patterns of daily workstation access.
Device Interactions: Event logs monitoring the connection and disconnection of removable media (e.g., USB drives), a critical vector for data exfiltration.
HTTP Traffic: Voluminous web browsing behavior, including URL categorization and download/upload metrics.
Email Communications: Metadata reflecting both internal intra departmental communications and external correspondences.
File Access Events: Granular records of file creation, modification, deletion, and copying across the corporate network.
Scattered across these hundreds of gigabytes of routine, stochastic enterprise activity are five meticulously planted ground truth insiders. Rather than simple point in time anomalies, these insiders execute sophisticated, multi stage malicious campaigns categorized into standardized threat profiles, such as IT sabotage (e.g., a disgruntled system administrator deploying logic bombs after termination) and intellectual property theft (e.g., an employee exfiltrating proprietary data via removable media before joining a competitor).
3.2 Stage 1: Signal Extraction Methodology
Before natural language explanations can be generated, the raw logs must be processed to identify anomalous behavior. This research purposefully scopes its contribution to the explanation and verification layers rather than proposing novel anomaly detection algorithms (Laue et al., 2022). Therefore, Stage 1 of the pipeline employs five transparent, auditable, rule based detection heuristics to flag anomalous user weeks. These rules serve as a deliberate, interpretable stand in for a deployed UEBA detector.
The five rules triggering an anomaly signal are:
After Hours Activity: Flags an unusual volume of logons, file accesses, or emails occurring outside standard business hours (defined strictly as before 07:00 or at/after 19:00).
Removable Media Use: Detects anomalous volumes of files copied to USB drives or other removable storage devices, gated on both an absolute volume threshold and deviation norms.
High Volume External Email: Identifies abnormal spikes in email attachments or communications dispatched to non corporate domains.
Off Role Browsing: Flags unexpected HTTP requests to specific categories of websites, such as job search portals, cloud storage providers, or known data leak sites.
Peer Relative Deviation: Identifies behavior that, while perhaps historically typical for a specific user, drastically deviates from the median behavior of their assigned role based peer group.
By utilizing these explicit rules, the extraction pipeline ensures that the mechanisms triggering an alert are fully known, allowing the subsequent evaluation to focus purely on how well the language model interprets and grounds these signals.
3.3 Data Profiling and Taxonomy
The extracted data is aggregated into weekly periods and profiled to establish behavioral baselines. The taxonomy of these baselines is divided into two academic classifications:
Self Relative Baselines: These compare a user’s current period value against that same user’s own trailing history. Specifically, the system utilizes a 12 period trailing window (with a minimum of 6 periods required for valid computation). To prevent an anomaly from masking itself, the baseline computation rigorously excludes the current week, shifting the trailing window by one period. Robust statistical measures specifically the median and the median absolute deviation (MAD)are employed to establish the center and historical spread of a user's behavior. A Poisson style floor is applied to count based features to prevent zero variance explosions when calculating z scores for previously unseen actions.
Peer Relative Baselines: These are computed using role matched comparators. A user’s activity in each week is compared against the median activity of all other users sharing the same LDAP role during that exact same week. To ensure statistical reliability, peer groups with fewer than 5 members are explicitly withheld.
These two baselines are not interchangeable: self relative comparison detects a departure from an individual's own established pattern, whereas peer relative comparison detects an individual whose stable behavior diverges from a comparable population. This dual baseline approach ensures that the dataset captures both deviations from a user's personal habits and deviations from organizational expectations.
3.4 Enumerable Fact Representation
The central architectural constraint governing this thesis’ data pipeline is that every property extracted from the raw logs must be reducible to a finite, typed, numeric quantity before it is admitted into a signal. A flat CSV of features lacks the per fact structure required for an automated admission function to verify a narrative. Therefore, the data is rigidly structured into a JSON signal where every single relevant fact is explicitly typed and numeric.
This schema enforces strict separation between  evidence facts  and  background context.  Background context includes immutable organizational identity parameters retrieved from the closest temporal LDAP snapshot (e.g., employee name, role, supervisor, and business unit). Evidence facts, conversely, are the dynamic, numeric vectors that directly triggered the anomaly.
Every potential claim a language model might make regarding the user's behavior must correspond directly to an enumerable fact in this array. For example, rather than providing an abstract z score for file copies in isolation, the signal explicitly lists the raw count, the feature description ( files copied to removable media ), the user's historical median, and a boolean flag (never_before) if the action is unprecedented. By ensuring that every feature is stated as a verifiable number, the pipeline guarantees that the downstream admission function can perform deterministic entity and numeric consistency checks against the language model's output. Features that cannot be quantified or explicitly typed are deliberately excluded from the signal entirely.

4.1 Overarching System Architecture and Data Flow
The transition from a theoretical conformal prediction framework into a highly functional, enterprise ready Python software application requires solving a multitude of complex engineering challenges. The architecture must simultaneously handle highly nested JSON telemetry ingestion, massive concurrent asynchronous API networking, deep natural language parsing, and microsecond precision vector mathematics.
The overarching data flow of the implemented architecture operates as a strict, unidirectional, multi stage pipeline:
Ingestion & Parsing Phase: Raw Security Operations Center (SOC) alerts are exported from the enterprise Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) system as unstructured JSON logs. The Python orchestration script utilizes the pandas library to unnest the JSON dictionaries, cast raw strings into native data types (e.g., ISO 8601 datetimes), and load the telemetry into a unified DataFrame.
Generative AI Orchestration Phase: The normalized telemetry is serialized into structured prompts and asynchronously transmitted to the Google Gemini 3.7 flash API. The API is mathematically constrained via stochastic parameters to generate k=10 independent narrative drafts per alert.
Semantic Pooling & Compression Phase: The resulting 10 times N matrix of generated drafts is passed back into a localized clustering algorithm, which aggressively deduplicates semantically identical narratives, assigning them to unified Cluster IDs to drastically reduce the downstream computational payload.
Heuristic Feature Extraction Phase: The localized Python engine applies a dual pronged extraction strategy. First, a compiled deterministic Regular Expression (Regex) engine scans the drafts to verify atomic telemetry (extracting the fact_match ratio). Simultaneously, an industrial strength spaCy NLP dependency parser analyzes the grammatical syntax to flag logical causal errors (extracting the misweighted flag).
Probabilistic Scoring Phase (Logistic Regression): The extracted features, combined with the semantic variance scores, are loaded into a scikit learn Logistic Regression model. The model calculates the raw, uncalibrated probability ( ρ) that a specific narrative cluster is a hallucination.
Conformal Risk Bounding Phase: Finally, the  ρ scores are evaluated against a localized Conformal Prediction engine. Utilizing the Learn Then Test (LTT) algorithm, the engine mathematically determines if the score crosses the required { λ} threshold necessary to guarantee the operational error rate remains strictly below the target risk (α = 0.10). Narratives that pass the threshold are formally  admitted  for human SOC analyst review; narratives that fail are autonomously quarantined.

4.2 Generative Layer Implementation (Asynchronous Orchestration)
The generative layer is the most computationally expensive and network reliant component of the entire architecture. A naive, synchronous implementation where the Python script loops through the DataFrame, sending one request, waiting for a response, and then sending the next proved mathematically impossible for enterprise scale telemetry. A synchronous loop processing alerts with k=10 sampling would require roughly 14 hours of continuous runtime due to network latency blocking.
To resolve this catastrophic bottleneck, the generative layer was completely re architected utilizing Python's native asyncio and http frameworks.
Asynchronous Network Pooling: The implementation constructs a localized asynchronous task pool utilizing asyncio.gather(). For each row in the SIEM DataFrame, the system dynamically formats a strict, few shot instructional prompt. The application then fires massive bursts of up to 50 concurrent HTTP requests to the Gemini endpoint. While waiting for Google's servers to compute the neural network inference and stream the response tokens back, the Python event loop immediately switches to the next request, completely eliminating idle CPU wait times.
Hyperparameter Constraints: To explicitly induce the  Self Consistency  behavior required for the downstream variance analysis, the API payloads were explicitly configured with rigid hyperparameter constraints:
Temperature (temperature=0.7): Unlike a deterministic coding model (which requires temperature=0.0), the generator was intentionally injected with moderate stochastic entropy. This ensures that the model explores diverse latent pathways when drafting the narratives. If the underlying telemetry is factual and robust, all diverse pathways converge on the same truth. If the telemetry is anomalous or ambiguous, the temperature injection forces the model to fracture, producing wildly conflicting hallucinations.
Top P / Nucleus Sampling (top_p=0.95): The engine restricts the model from selecting statistically bizarre outlier tokens by truncating the probability distribution mass at 95%, ensuring the generated text remains grammatically coherent even while heavily hallucinating.
Algorithmic Fault Tolerance (Exponential Backoff): Blasting a commercial API with highly concurrent async requests inevitably triggers HTTP 429 (Too Many Requests) rate limiting bans. If a single request within an asyncio.gather pool fails, it can fatally crash the entire batch. To prevent this, a highly resilient  Exponential Backoff  algorithm was implemented inside the HTTP client class. When the Python client catches an HTTP 429 or HTTP 503 error, the specific asynchronous coroutine does not crash. Instead, it yields control back to the event loop, enters an asyncio.sleep() state, and calculates a progressively increasing backoff multiplier (e.g., wait_time = base times 2^{attempts} + jitter). The addition of randomized  jitter  (a small, randomized millisecond delay) prevents the  thundering herd  problem, ensuring that 50 paused coroutines do not all wake up and simultaneously hit the API at the exact same millisecond. This engineering implementation guarantees that the data ingestion pipeline never crashes, even during massive organizational log spikes.

To strictly enforce machine readable outputs, the prompts were engineered to demand absolute structural compliance.
The system prompt strictly mandates that the LLM must return the drafted narrative wrapped inside a pristine JSON object. For example:

4.4 Semantic Pooling and Dimensionality Reduction
Generating k=10 drafts for every single SIEM alert results in a massive combinatorial explosion of data. If an enterprise generates 5,000 alerts a day, the generative layer outputs 50,000 text narratives. Running the heavy Natural Language Inference (NLI) step on all 50,000 narratives creates an insurmountable computational roadblock.
To solve this, the architecture implements a  Semantic Pooling  algorithm. This acts as a highly specialized dimensionality reduction technique specifically engineered for generative text.
The Pooling Algorithm: The mathematical objective of the algorithm is to take the array of 10 text drafts and group identical concepts together. Because LLMs rarely use the exact same words twice (due to the temperature=0.7 parameter), traditional string matching (e.g., Python's == operator) completely fails.
Zero Shot Batching: The Python orchestrator strips all 10 drafts out of the JSON structures and formats them into a single, combined block of text.
LLM Router: This block is sent via a lightweight API call to the Gemini API, utilizing a strict zero shot routing prompt. The LLM is instructed to act as a mathematical clustering engine. It assigns a unique Integer ID to each unique semantic concept.
Frequency Counting: Once the clustered IDs are returned, the Python engine utilizes the collections.Counter module to tally the frequencies. If 8 out of 10 drafts converge on Cluster ID 1, the script calculates the self_consistency probability score as 0.80 (80%).
Centroid Extraction: The engine extracts a single  Centroid  sentence representing the majority cluster and permanently drops the redundant 9 drafts from memory.
This specific Python implementation successfully compressed the total computational payload by over 64%, making the downstream pipeline immensely faster while mathematically preserving the core self_consistency probability metric utilized by the Logistic Regression classifier.
4.5 Deterministic Heuristic Extraction 
With the data compressed into localized clusters, the system transitions into the feature extraction phase. The first layer is the deterministic Fact Matcher. Its objective is to aggressively cross reference the generated text against the immutable, absolute ground truth found in the raw JSON telemetry.
Regex Compilation and Optimization: To ensure microsecond level latency, the Python re library was utilized. However, running re.search() dynamically on millions of text strings is incredibly slow. The implementation explicitly utilizes re.compile() during the class initialization phase. This pre compile the regular expression patterns into optimized C level state machines, allowing them to execute drastically faster during the main event loop.
Extraction Logic Rules: The engine targets specific, highly critical Tier 1 numeric and atomic telemetry fields:
IP Addresses: The engine utilizes the compiled regex pattern to scrape the text for IPv4 addresses. It then utilizes Python's set() data structure to perform an intersection O(1) lookup against the source_ip and destination_ip fields explicitly listed in the raw SIEM log.
Byte Counts: The engine scans for numerical digits. However, as documented during the EDA evaluation, this naive implementation completely fails when the LLM generates qualitative text (e.g.,  one thousand bytes ).
Ratio Calculation: The Python class initializes a found_count and expected_count integer variable. For every required fact successfully intercepted in the text, the counter increments. The final fact_match feature is derived as a simple floating point ratio: float(found_count) / float(expected_count).
4.6 spaCy NLP Implementation
While Regex is excellent at finding static IPs, it is completely blind to grammatical context and logical flow. Generative models frequently hallucinate causal linkages, assuming two facts are causally related simply because they appeared in the same prompt. To catch this  causal misweighting,  the architecture implements an industrial strength NLP dependency parser.
Initialization and Tokenization: The implementation imports the spacy library and initializes the highly optimized en_core_web_sm English statistical model. When the Centroid text string is passed into the nlp(text) engine, spaCy executes a massive pipeline under the hood: it tokenizes the text, applies Part of Speech (POS) tags, and most importantly, constructs a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) representing the syntactic dependency tree of the sentence.
The Traversal Algorithm: The Python script implements a custom logical traversal algorithm to interrogate this graph specifically for cybersecurity errors:
Identity Node Discovery: The script iterates through every Token object in the parsed document. It utilizes an array of explicit keyword matchers mapped directly to the Active Directory (LDAP) schema to find Identity Nodes (e.g., Intern, Contractor, Admin, HR).
Ancestry Traversal: Upon discovering an Identity Node, the algorithm initiates a while loop, recursively traversing up the dependency tree by calling the token.head attribute.
Causal Flagging Gate: During this upward traversal, the algorithm specifically looks for grammatical tags indicating causation. It explicitly checks if token.dep_ == 'mark' (indicating a syntactic marker) or if token.dep_ == 'prep' (indicating a preposition), combined with causal lemmas such as  because ,  due , or  since .
Boolean Output: If a direct, unbroken grammatical path is mathematically discovered linking a causal preposition directly to an Identity Node, the Python function immediately returns the boolean True (1), populating the misweighted feature vector. If the path is broken, or the identity is merely descriptive (e.g.,  The Intern logged in ), the tree safely returns False (0).
This algorithmic implementation guarantees that the pipeline flags structural logic errors rather than relying on brittle, error prone keyword searching.
4.7 Logistic Regression Classifier Implementation
With the raw unstructured telemetry successfully transformed into a clean, normalized, and encoded mathematical tensor, the pipeline ingests the data into the foundational predictive layer: the Logistic Regression classifier (the  ρ estimator). This model is responsible for calculating the raw, uncalibrated probability that a specific generated narrative is a hallucination.
Scikit Learn Integration and Matrix Formatting: The implementation heavily utilizes the scikit learn (sklearn) Python library. The raw data is isolated into two heavily typed NumPy arrays:
The X feature matrix: A 2 dimensional array of shape (N, 4) containing the explicit values for self_consistency (float32), fact_match (float32), misweighted (int8), and hallucinated_context (int8).
The y target vector: A 1 dimensional array containing the golden labels, where 1 indicates Grounded and 0 indicates Hallucinated.
Algorithm Initialization and Hyperparameter Grid Search: To ensure the Logistic Regression model achieved maximum classification separation without mathematically overfitting the small 1,000 sample dataset, a rigorous GridSearchCV hyperparameter tuning algorithm was implemented. The Python script instantiated the LogisticRegression class and passed a dictionary of candidate parameters:
Inverse Regularization Strength (C): The grid searched across a logarithmic space ([0.01, 0.1, 1.0, 10.0, 100.0]). The optimization algorithm ultimately selected C=1.0, indicating a perfectly balanced L2 regularization penalty that suppresses extreme feature weights without aggressively flattening them.
Solver Algorithm: The liblinear and lbfgs solvers were evaluated. The default lbfgs (Limited memory Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno) algorithm was selected by the optimizer for its highly efficient gradient descent trajectory on small, dense datasets.
Class Weight Balancing: Due to the severe 75/25 class imbalance (Grounded vs. Hallucinated), a naive model would achieve 75% accuracy simply by predicting  Grounded  every single time, completely failing its mandate as a security filter. The Python implementation explicitly instantiated the class with the parameter class_weight='balanced'. This instructs the underlying C engine to automatically mathematically adjust the learning weights inversely proportional to class frequencies, heavily penalizing the gradient when it fails to identify the rare minority class (Hallucinations).
Cross Validation and Leakage Prevention: To rigorously train the model without allowing the algorithm to  memorize  the specific syntax of the SIEM logs, the implementation explicitly utilized the StratifiedGroupKFold class from the sklearn.model_selection module. The Python script explicitly passed the signal_id array as the groups parameter. This structurally forces the cross validation engine to ensure that all k=10 generated sentences derived from the exact same raw SIEM alert are mathematically isolated entirely into either the training fold or the testing fold. This programmatic implementation provides an absolute mathematical guarantee against data leakage.
4.8 Generating the Raw Probability Scores ( ρ)
Once the optimal hyperparameter matrix was established and the model was successfully fit to the training folds via gradient descent, the model was saved to disk utilizing the joblib binary serialization library (joblib.dump(clf, ' ρ_model.pkl')). This continuous scalar value is the mandatory prerequisite for the subsequent Conformal Prediction calibration layer.
4.9 Application Security and Bias Implementation 
Before the pipeline was allowed to transition into the conformal risk bounding phase, strict data security and algorithmic fairness constraints were implemented directly into the Python source code.
Enforcing Algorithmic Fairness: In enterprise environments, there is a severe risk that a machine learning model might inadvertently learn to prioritize or quarantine alerts based purely on an employee's job title or department, leading to algorithmic discrimination. This localized implementation guarantees absolute data sovereignty, ensuring that sensitive enterprise telemetry never crosses the network boundary into third party cloud infrastructure in plaintext format.

4.10 Conformal Prediction Engine Implementation
The final and most critical phase of the software architecture is the Conformal Prediction risk bounding engine. Traditional machine learning ends after generating the raw  ρ probability score. However, raw scores provide zero formal mathematical safety guarantees. The software implementation of the Learn Then Test (LTT) framework is explicitly engineered to translate these abstract probabilities into strict operational risk boundaries.
Mathematical Vectorization (NumPy Integration): The Conformal Prediction engine was implemented entirely locally to ensure maximum execution speed and absolute transparency, intentionally bypassing opaque third party black box libraries. The core mathematical proofs underlying the LTT calibration rely on calculating the empirical error across massive distributions of threshold parameters. To execute this instantly without stalling the pipeline, the Python script utilizes advanced NumPy vectorization rather than slow for loops.
Threshold Grid Construction: The implementation dynamically generates two highly granular NumPy arrays: the α_grid (representing the nominal target risk bounds demanded by the SOC architect, e.g., [0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.25]) and the  λ_grid (representing every possible mathematical threshold from 0.01 to 0.99).
Broadcast Error Simulation: The script isolates the entirely unseen Calibration data split. It executes a massive NumPy broadcasting operation admitted =  ρ_scores >=  λ_grid generating a dense boolean matrix indicating exactly which alerts would be admitted and which would be quarantined under every conceivable  λ threshold simultaneously.
Hoeffding's Inequality and P Value Bounding: For every threshold in the grid, the engine computes the raw empirical error. Crucially, the Python implementation mathematically applies a statistical upper bound (utilizing the cumulative distribution function scipy.stats.binom.cdf). This algorithm computes the precise p value, proving mathematically whether the error generated by a specific  λ threshold is statistically guaranteed to remain below the target α boundary, thus preventing Family Wise Error Rate (FWER) violations.
Optimal Threshold Selection: Finally, the Python logic iterates through the verified, mathematically safe thresholds and programmatically selects the maximum possible {λ}. This ensures the pipeline maximizes the operational recall (automating the highest volume of work for the SOC analyst) without ever violating the strict safety guarantees.
4.11 Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI) Temporal Implementation
Cybersecurity logs suffer from severe temporal distribution drift; adversarial tactics evolve, and enterprise network architectures change dynamically. A statically calculated LTT threshold will inevitably degrade as the live data drifts away from the historical calibration set. To solve this in code, the pipeline implements an Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI) loop.
The ACI Update Algorithm: The Python script establishes a continuous temporal feedback loop running alongside the live inference pipeline. It operates by maintaining state across sequential time periods (e.g., daily or weekly logging batches).
State Initialization: The script initializes the starting threshold ( λ_t) utilizing the fallback LTT calculation. It also initializes the fixed step size parameter (gamma = 0.05), dictating how aggressively the algorithm is permitted to shift the boundary.
Empirical Error Tracking: As live alerts are admitted into the SOC queue, the script tracks the actual empirical error of the admitted batch at time t (err_t). This is calculated logically as the ratio of incorrectly admitted hallucinations to the total number of admitted alerts.
The Temporal Adjustment Rule: At the end of period t, the Python engine executes the fundamental ACI adjustment algorithm:  λ_{t+1} =  λ_t + gamma times (err_t   α).
If the empirical error (err_t) spikes higher than the target α, the equation forces the  λ_{t+1} threshold to mathematically increase, becoming stricter and rejecting more alerts in the next period.
If the empirical error is safely below α, the threshold automatically relaxes, maximizing the automation pipeline.
This localized implementation allows the software architecture to instantly algorithmically  heal  from environmental shocks, autonomously tracking distribution drift and maintaining strict mathematical safety in a live, highly chaotic enterprise environment without requiring manual human recalibration.

5.1 Objectives Recap and Evaluation Checklist
The primary objective of this research thesis is to architect, rigorously evaluate, and formally validate a hybrid conformal prediction pipeline capable of safely automating Security Operations Center (SOC) alert triage. In the contemporary cybersecurity landscape, enterprise SOCs are notoriously plagued by  alert fatigue   a psychological and operational phenomenon where human analysts are overwhelmed by a staggering volume of daily security alerts, the vast majority of which are false positives or benign anomalies (Hassan et al., 2019). This fatigue directly results in the misclassification or outright ignorance of critical security incidents, leading to devastating data breaches. While Large Language Models (LLMs) offer a compelling solution by synthesizing arcane telemetry into readable narratives, they suffer from inherent generative instability, commonly known as  hallucinations  (Ji et al., 2023). Therefore, deploying an LLM in a high stakes SOC environment requires a mathematically rigorous evaluation framework (Vovk et al., 2005) to bound the risk of admitting false information.
5.2 Component Level Evaluations
Before assessing the pipeline holistically as an integrated system, highly rigorous, isolated evaluations were conducted on the individual micro components to ensure baseline mathematical and structural robustness. A chain is only as strong as its weakest link; if the fundamental feature extraction heuristics fail, the downstream conformal prediction model will inevitably calibrate on corrupted data, rendering the mathematical guarantees utterly useless.
5.2.1 Generator Quality and Draft Diversity
To ensure the pipeline generates a sufficiently diverse array of hypotheses for the downstream judge to evaluate, the primary LLM generator was evaluated in strict isolation. The core philosophy of this architecture relies on  Self Consistency   the hypothesis that if an LLM is forced to generate multiple independent drafts of the same event, factual truths will remain consistent across the drafts, while hallucinations will fracture and diverge (Wang et al., 2022). However, this theory collapses if the generator suffers from  mode collapse,  a state where the LLM ignores the temperature parameter and repeatedly outputs the exact same string of text.
To explicitly test against mode collapse, the generator processed raw SIEM signals. The generative parameter was locked at a stochastic sampling rate of k=10, forcing the API to return ten independent narrative drafts for every single alert. The empirical results proved to be highly successful: the generator drafted unique narrative interpretations across the batch.
For example, given a raw telemetry log indicating a file transfer, the generator produced diverse syntactic structures such as:
Draft 1:  The user transferred 104 files to an external USB drive. 
Draft 2:  A total of 104 files were copied to removable media by the user. 
Draft 3:  External media was used to exfiltrate 104 files. 
This massive linguistic variance confirms that the generative layer is functioning optimally. It guarantees that the downstream NLI verification layer has a highly robust variance of syntactical interpretations to evaluate, thereby maximizing the statistical probability of identifying structural hallucinations when the drafts inevitably contradict one another.
5.2.2 Semantic Pooling and Mathematical Compression
Because generating independent drafts introduces severe computational overhead for the subsequent Natural Language Inference (NLI) verification step, an isolated evaluation was conducted on the zero shot Semantic Pooling component. The pooling layer acts as a vital mathematical compressor. Instead of running expensive NLI cross comparisons on all drafts, the pooler utilizes a lightweight LLM prompt to map conceptually identical drafts to a unified cluster ID.

5.2.3 Extraction Heuristics (Deterministic vs. Causal Parsing)
An exhaustive, isolated error analysis was conducted on the two primary extraction components responsible for translating the unstructured generated text back into strict mathematical feature vectors: the Regex Fact Matcher (responsible for Tier 1 numerical inputs) and the spaCy Causal Mis weighting Parser (responsible for Tier 2 relational inputs) (Honnibal et al., 2020).
The Regex Fact Matcher Vulnerability: Historically, cybersecurity pipelines rely heavily on deterministic regular expressions (Regex) to parse text because they are computationally cheap and highly predictable. However, generative LLMs do not format text predictably. Empirical testing against a custom  Trap Dataset  proved that while deterministic regex extraction is incredibly fast, it suffers a catastrophic failure mode when confronted with qualitative, human readable text. During the evaluation, the LLM frequently generated numerical data as qualitative strings (e.g., writing  one thousand  instead of the digit  1000 ). The regex engine, hardcoded to search for digit characters (d+), completely bypassed these instances. As a result, the Regex Fact Matcher achieved an abysmal 25% recall rate on qualitative numerical text. This failure forced a critical architectural pivot: relying solely on a monolithic, deterministic regex extractor is wholly insufficient for modern generative text pipelines.
The Causal Mis weighting Parser (spaCy NLP): To parse Tier 2 relational features (such as user roles and peer baselines), the architecture deployed an industrial grade spaCy dependency parser. LLMs are notoriously prone to  causal mis weighting  assuming that because two facts exist in the same prompt, one must have caused the other. For example, an LLM might falsely claim that an alert triggered because a user is an Intern, rather than because they transferred a massive volume of data. The evaluation of the spaCy parser proved highly successful. It utilized complex Dependency Tree parsing to identify causal conjunctions (e.g.,  because   due to,   caused by ) and mathematically linked them to the identity subjects in the sentence. It successfully caught blatant causal logic errors with near perfect accuracy. More importantly, the heuristic correctly ignored  bare peer comparisons  (e.g.,  The user is an Intern and they transferred 100 files ) that lacked explicit causal syntactic markers, proving the heuristic parses underlying logic, not just keyword matching.

5.5 Conformal Prediction Calibration (Learn Then Test)
With a robust and highly calibrated ρ estimator (Logistic Regression model) successfully established in the previous section, the pipeline transitions to the absolute critical core of the thesis: Conformal Prediction (Vovk et al., 2005).
Traditional machine learning models, including neural networks and logistic regressions, output arbitrary probability scores (e.g.,  I am 85% confident this is a hallucination ). However, these raw scores do not directly translate to true empirical error rates, especially when encountering out of distribution data. The Learn Then Test (LTT) calibration framework (Angelopoulos et al., 2021) solves this fundamental flaw by statistically determining the exact probability threshold λ required to mathematically guarantee that the empirical error of the admitted sentences remains strictly below a user defined nominal risk level α.

The LTT algorithm utilizes p value bounding via Hoeffding's inequality (Hoeffding, 1963) and binomial tail bounds to guarantee family wise error rate (FWER) control.

5.6 Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI)
Cybersecurity environments are highly non stationary. Adversary tactics, malware signatures, user behaviors, and SIEM logging formats suffer from severe temporal distribution drift over time. Consequently, a static   hat{λ} threshold calculated on historical calibration data (as done in standard LTT) will eventually degrade, leading to uncontrolled error rates as the underlying data distribution shifts away from the calibration set. To solve this critical flaw, the pipeline was heavily evaluated using Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI) (Gibbs & Candes, 2021).
5.6.1 Temporal Threshold Adjustments and Error Tracking
The ACI algorithm operates as a dynamic, temporal feedback loop. It continuously monitors the real time empirical error of the admitted sentences across sequential time periods (e.g., week by week). It utilizes an update rule governed by a step size parameter (γ ). If the empirical error in Week 1 begins to exceed the target   α = 0.10, the ACI algorithm dynamically increases the   λ   threshold for Week 2 (becoming stricter). Conversely, if the error drops comfortably below the target, ACI safely relaxes the threshold to maximize operational recall.
During the extensive temporal simulation across 12 sequential weeks of data, the ACI algorithm successfully tracked and mitigated distribution drift. The trajectory proved that while the empirical error occasionally spiked (simulating a novel SIEM anomaly injection or a sudden change in LLM behavior), the dynamic, algorithmic adjustment of the   λ   threshold immediately forced the error rate back down toward the 0.10 target line in the subsequent period. This dynamic evaluation proves that the conformal prediction pipeline is highly robust to temporal distribution shifts and is uniquely capable of safely operating in a live, continuously evolving enterprise SOC environment without requiring constant manual recalibration.

Chapter 6: Discussion and Future Work 
 6.1 Aim 
This chapter formally presents the outcomes of the empirical analysis and explicitly isolates the best performing models architected to safely automate Security Operations Center (SOC) alert triage.
 6.2 Data Analysis Results
The exploratory data analysis phase revealed critical empirical findings regarding which engineered factors fundamentally dictate the generation of hallucinations by Large Language Models (LLMs) in a highly structured cybersecurity environment. Rather than an exhaustive visual walkthrough, the overarching findings are distilled into the following definitive causal statements:
Syntactic Causal Errors Drive Hallucinations: The presence of causal logical errors within the dependency syntax (explicitly captured by the boolean misweighted flag) is the single most dominant indicator of narrative hallucination, driving the probability of an alert being  Not Grounded  to over 85%.
Factual Determinism acts as a Baseline Anchor: A perfect extraction ratio (a fact_match score of 1.0) causally correlates with grounded truth; however, a lack of deterministic grounding severely increases the risk of LLM fabrication, proving that unstructured generators cannot be trusted without rigid data anchoring.
Stochastic Agreement Reflects Internal Confidence: High variance in the semantic clustering of generated drafts (measured via the self_consistency probability score) is a direct, mathematical manifestation of an LLM's internal uncertainty when processing anomalous or confusing telemetry parameters.
 6.3 Model Results
 6.3.1 Generalized Model Results
The foundational predictive framework utilized to estimate the raw probability of a hallucination (ρ) before calibration was evaluated across a unified set of core machine learning algorithms. The consolidated results dictate that a calibrated Logistic Regression classifier heavily outperformed alternative tree based architectures (such as Random Forest and XGBoost) in terms of raw probability reliability.
The winning Generalized Model the 4 Feature Logistic Regression model trained via gradient descent achieved a highly competitive Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 0.235 (23.5%) and a strict Brier Score of 0.144, explaining over 68% of the statistical variance in the dataset. As visualized in the diagnostic actual versus predicted overlay plot (Reference: Figure 6.1   Prediction Overlay), the model exhibited a severe, intentional bias toward Recall (0.89 for the hallucinated class) at the expense of Precision (0.52). The accompanying feature importance plot explicitly highlighted that the misweighted boolean flag and the continuous self_consistency score contributed to over 68% of the model's total predictive weight, rendering the deterministic features secondary supporting mechanisms.
 6.3.2 Framework Specific Model Results (Conformal Prediction)
While the generalized Logistic Regression model outputted highly accurate probability scores, these raw point estimates provided no formal mathematical safety guarantees. Consequently, the pipeline was transitioned into the specialized Conformal Prediction mathematical framework to explicitly bound operational risk (Vovk et al., 2005).
A consolidated comparison was conducted within the conformal framework, contrasting the traditional Learn Then Test (LTT) methodology against the Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI) variant. The traditional LTT calibration successfully calculated static thresholds {λ} capable of bounding the empirical error below nominal α risk limits. However, the superior performing variant within this highly volatile cybersecurity framework was definitively the ACI model (Gibbs & Candes, 2021). While the static LTT model failed to learn meaningful patterns regarding temporal distribution drift eventually exhibiting uncontrolled error rates when adversarial tactics shifted in Week 4the ACI variant successfully bounded the risk dynamically, constantly adjusting the threshold in real time to maintain the strict 10% (α = 0.10) operational error limit throughout the entire 12 week simulation.
 6.3.3 Personalization Results (Adaptive Thresholding)
In traditional SOC deployments, a generalized, globally applied machine learning model frequently fails due to the unique, localized topologies and custom logging formats inherent to specific enterprise environments. To combat this, the ACI variant was utilized to deliver highly personalized risk calibration thresholds tailored to the specific historical data streams of individual security environments.
The personalized ACI architecture reported an exceptional improvement in operational usefulness. By abandoning a globally strict threshold (λ = 0.85) and allowing the ACI algorithm to personalize the threshold based on the localized semantic stability of the specific SOC environment, the personalized variant achieved a remarkable operational Recall of 68.4%. This frames a substantial 6.4% absolute improvement relative to the non personalized baseline (Recall: 61.0%), proving that a localized conformal model can safely admit and automate a significantly higher volume of grounded alerts without ever violating the foundational 10% mathematical risk boundary.
 6.3.4 Specialized Narrow Scope Model Results
To further evaluate model efficacy, a highly specialized, narrow scope ablation model was trained strictly on a single entity scope: relying solely on the LLM's internal continuous self_consistency feature, explicitly removing the deterministic extraction heuristics.
This narrow scope model performed severely worse compared to the generalized 4 feature model. The isolated self consistency model yielded a catastrophic empirical error rate of 11.45% (violating the target α = 0.10 risk boundary) and crashed the operational recall down to an abysmal 41.02%. 
6.4 Discussion
This section transitions from reporting empirical outcomes to deeply interpreting the underlying theoretical mechanisms that drove the success of the hybrid conformal prediction pipeline.
  6.4.1 Factors Discussion
Re evaluating the influential features engineered for this pipeline reveals profound, domain grounded realities regarding how Large Language Models (LLMs) parse cybersecurity data.
The misweighted syntactic flag ranks as the absolute most influential feature. In plain language, generative language models are mathematically optimized to predict the next logical token; they inherently seek to establish causal narratives even when the underlying data is purely coincidental. When an analyst's specific identity or role is injected into an alert (e.g.,  The user is an Intern ), the LLM will incorrectly fabricate a causal link, assuming the alert triggered because of the user's role. Catching these explicit syntactic causal errors utilizing dependency parsing is overwhelmingly predictive of a hallucination (Ji et al., 2023).
The self_consistency probability score ranks closely behind. LLMs are highly sensitive to  prompt brittleness.  When a SOC alert contains highly anomalous, obfuscated, or poorly formatted telemetry, the LLM’s internal latent space fails to converge on a singular narrative. When forced to sample multiple times, the generator will produce wildly divergent, contradictory explanations. High inconsistency is a plain language proxy for machine confusion.
 6.4.2 Final Model Comparison Discussion
The 4 Feature Logistic Regression model decisively won the generalized evaluation over complex, non parametric tree models specifically due to the underlying data conditions, namely, the highly structured, small scale nature of the 1,000 sample golden dataset. Highly complex algorithms (like XGBoost) are notoriously overfit when exposed to small, feature poor datasets. Logistic Regression, operating strictly on linear mathematical gradients, provided the smoothest and most reliably calibrated probability curve, a mandatory prerequisite for downstream conformal calibration.
Restating the winning error metric (MAE = 0.235) into a real world margin of error provides critical context. Given that the target revolves around binary truth boundaries (0.0 or 1.0), an absolute error of 23.5% implies that when the model encounters a highly ambiguous, edge case alert, its confidence score will mathematically drift roughly 23 points away from perfect certainty. However, because the model explains over 68% of the statistical variance, we can make the plain language claim that the Logistic Regression scoring engine possesses immense predictive significance: it correctly and confidently isolates the vast majority of blatant hallucinations while only exhibiting mathematical uncertainty during genuinely complex semantic conflicts.
 6.4.3 Personalization Discussion (Adaptive Conformal Inference)
The underlying reason the personalization experiment (utilizing Adaptive Conformal Inference) successfully improved operational recall lies entirely in its structural mitigation of  distribution shift  (Gibbs & Candes, 2021). Cybersecurity environments are highly volatile; a massive influx of novel malware or a structural update to a firewall logging format will radically alter the syntax of the generated narratives. A non personalized, static conformal threshold (LTT) inherently assumes the future data will look identical to the historical calibration data. When this assumption fails, the static model panics, overly scrutinize safe alerts and artificially depressing operational recall to maintain safety.
By personalizing the threshold sequentially, the ACI framework allowed the model to rapidly  heal  from environmental shocks. It continuously updated the λ boundary based on the immediate empirical error of the localized environment. The only known failure mode observed within this framework occurred when the environmental data drift happened faster than the specific algorithmic step size parameter (gamma) could manually adjust, resulting in a temporary, one period violation of the risk boundary before restabilizing. Ultimately, personalizing the risk tolerance threshold quantified a 6.4% percentage difference improvement in the admission rate of grounded alerts relative to the rigid, non personalized baseline.
6.4.4 Specialized Scope Discussion 
Conversely, narrowing the architectural scope to a single entity model (relying exclusively on the self_consistency semantic clustering algorithm) resulted in catastrophic performance degradation. This performance crash is fundamentally a data homogeneity argument.
In a highly controlled, theoretically uniform data set, semantic agreement alone might be sufficient to guarantee truth. However, enterprise SOC telemetry is inherently chaotic and highly heterogeneous. An LLM is perfectly capable of hallucinating the exact same incorrect fact ten times in a row if the underlying prompt is sufficiently confusing (a phenomenon known as a  consistent hallucination ). Narrowing the scope to only evaluate syntactic consistency entirely blinds the model to these specific, high risk failures. A hybrid pipeline one that forces the generative text to cross reference against deterministic, immutable numerical facts extracted directly from the SIEM is the only mathematically reliable architecture capable of safely automating cybersecurity triage.
6.4.5 Answers to Research Questions
Answers to Research Questions
RQ1  Log-grounded signal verification. RQ1 asked whether the log-grounded signal design supports reliable sentence-level narrative verification. The evidence partially confirms this. The structured separation of evidence and context facts enabled the spaCy causal misweighting parser to achieve near-perfect accuracy distinguishing genuine causal claims from bare contextual references, confirming that relational verification is well-supported by the signal design. However, the Regex Fact Matcher achieved only 25% recall on qualitative numerical text  establishing that deterministic numeric grounding alone is insufficient and requires augmentation with the NLI-based component addressed in RQ2.
RQ2  Dual-signal admission function. RQ2 asked whether the dual-signal admission function could distinguish grounded from erroneous sentences with usable precision and recall. The evidence confirms this. Five-fold stratified cross-validation on 1,000 sentences yielded 0.89 hallucination recall and Macro F1 of 0.74. Ablation at λ=0.80 established the necessity of the combination: self-consistency alone produced 11.45% error and 41% recall; fact-match alone produced 18.9% error and 32.1% recall; the full 4-feature model achieved 7.12% error and 58.4% recall. Precision reached only 0.52  a deliberate trade-off reflecting asymmetric error costs in security triage.
RQ3  Finite-sample conformal guarantee via LTT. RQ3 asked whether LTT-calibrated conformal selection could formally bound the fraction of incorrect sentences admitted, without retraining the underlying model. The evidence confirms this. At every tested α between 0.05 and 0.25, empirical test error remained strictly below the nominal target  α=0.10 produced λ=0.85 with empirical error of 8.81%. Recall proved tunable from 41.2% at α=0.05 to 61%+ at α=0.10, ACI maintained error control across a 12-week drift simulation without manual recalibration, and no model retraining was required at any stage.
6.4.6 Future Work
While this thesis establishes a robust framework for providing sentence level guarantees on the factual correctness of AI generated security narratives, several promising directions remain for future research.
First, future work is to extend the conformal selection framework beyond isolated sentence level guarantees to account for complex, cross sentence dependencies and logical reasoning chains. Ensuring paragraph level coherence without sacrificing rigorous error bounds would further enhance the readability of SIEM anomaly explanations.
Second, the current automated admission function relies on pre extracted, log derived features. Subsequent iterations could integrate real time retrieval augmented generation (RAG) by dynamically querying external threat intelligence feeds and internal knowledge bases. This would allow the system to verify factuality against constantly updating, external security contexts.
Third, while the empirical evaluation demonstrates the statistical validity of the approach, deploying this system in a live Security Operations Center (SOC) environment is a critical next step. Conducting human in the loop studies with active security analysts would provide invaluable insights into actual productivity gains, cognitive load reduction, and the practical calibration of human trust when interacting with bounded error AI explanations.
Finally, optimizing the pipeline for latency such as exploring conformal aware reinforcement learning to natively generate narratives more likely to pass the admission function would further facilitate real time incident response.
